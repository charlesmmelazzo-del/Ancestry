Pvt. Alexander Henry Quesenberry, Sr.

1842–1863

Killed at Chancellorsville on the third of May, 1863 — the same battle that mortally wounded Stonewall Jackson. His widow Rebecca was carrying their son when he died; the boy was born three months later.

Documented

Crockett Quisenberry (1822–1902) Nancy J. Quesenberry (1820–1901) │ ▼ Pvt. Alexander Henry Quesenberry (1842–1863) ⚭ Rebecca Jane Smyth Quesenberry Travis │ ▼ Alexander Henry Quesenberry, Jr. (1863–1937)
Military Service Confederate Private — KILLED IN ACTION at the Battle of Chancellorsville, 3 May 1863. Aged 21.

Pvt. Alexander Henry Quesenberry, Sr. was born on the 6th of January, 1842, in Washington County, Virginia — the far southwest corner of the state, in the high mountain country bordering Tennessee. He was twenty when he married Rebecca Jane Smyth (1844–1920) in or around 1862. Whether they had even a year together as husband and wife is uncertain — what is certain is that by the spring of 1863 he was a Confederate Private somewhere in the Army of Northern Virginia, and Rebecca was carrying their first and only child.

On the 3rd of May, 1863, he was killed at the Battle of Chancellorsville, in Spotsylvania County, Virginia — Robert E. Lee's greatest tactical victory, and the battle in which Stonewall Jackson was mortally wounded by his own men two nights before. Some 17,000 Union soldiers and 13,000 Confederates fell over those three days. Pvt. Alexander Henry Quesenberry was one of them, dead at age twenty-one.

His son, Alexander Henry Quesenberry, Jr., was born on the 29th of July, 1863 — almost three months after his father's death — in Jonesville, Lee County, Virginia, where Rebecca had taken refuge with kin. The boy never met his father, and grew up in the household of his widowed teenage mother. Every Quesenberry descendant in Mike's line owes their existence to this young soldier who fell at Chancellorsville.

His regimental affiliation and burial site remain open research questions. Confederate dead at Chancellorsville were buried in mass graves on the battlefield; many were later moved to the Confederate Cemetery at Fredericksburg or to home cemeteries when families could afford to retrieve them. Whether Alexander Henry's body returned to Washington or Lee County, or rests still in the Wilderness woods, is unknown.
