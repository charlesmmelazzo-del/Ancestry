Quesenberry Line — Generation 4

John Quesenberry Sr.

1790–1853

Patriarch of Greasy Creek — and, by Civil War's end, the only American father documented to have sent nine sons into Confederate service.

✓ Documented from public records

John Quesenberry Sr. was born in 1790 at Greasy Creek, in what would in 1831 become Floyd County, Virginia — one of those Blue Ridge valleys whose names (Greasy Creek, Indian Valley, Burks Fork) still feel like the frontier they were. He was the son of George Quesenberry, who had served in the Montgomery County, Virginia Militia during the American Revolution, and Martha 'Patsy' Slinker.

He married Nancy Hylton (1803 – after 1860), of the great Hylton family that had been among the very first white settlers of Indian Valley. Together they had fourteen children. The federal census of 1840 records their household at fourteen souls; by 1850 it numbered twelve, with ten children at home.

And then, in the year that John died — 1853 — the country slid toward the war that would define his sons' lives. When the Civil War came eight years later, all nine of John's surviving sons enlisted in the Confederate Army: George Washington Quesenberry, Elijah Quesenberry, Thomas Frederick Quesenberry, Archelaus Quesenberry, Nathaniel Floyd Quesenberry, John Quesenberry Jr., William Henry Clay Quesenberry, Amos Ballard Quesenberry, and James Montgomery Quesenberry. They served variously in the 54th Virginia Infantry, the 49th Virginia Infantry (Company G), and other regiments raised in the southwestern counties. The Quesenberry Family Papers at the Virginia Museum of History & Culture preserve the 1862 muster roll. No other American family on documentary record sent nine sons into Confederate service.

John Quesenberry Sr. did not live to see it. He died in Wythe County in 1853, and lies — by family tradition — somewhere in the high country between Greasy Creek and the New River.
