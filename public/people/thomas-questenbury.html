Quesenberry Line — Generation 9

Thomas Questenbury

1608–1672

The Atlantic crossing of 1624 — Thomas Questenbury, sixteen years old, a passenger to colonial Virginia. From him every American Quesenberry, Quisenberry, and Christenberry descends.

On a wooden ship sometime in the year 1624, a sixteen-year-old boy named Thomas Questenbury sailed from the south of England — most likely from Gravesend or one of the Kentish ports — to the seventeen-year-old colony of Virginia. He had been born in 1608 in Bromley, in the county of Kent, the son of James Questenbury and Joan Lydes. The Jamestown settlement was barely a generation old when he stepped ashore.

He survived. He married in Virginia and fathered at least two sons — John and Francis — who remained in the colony and became the patriarchs of the American Quesenberry families. By 1650, Thomas appears to have returned to England, settling in Canterbury and contracting a second marriage there. He died at Canterbury in 1672, at the age of about sixty-four.

But his sons stayed. From his son John Quisenberry (1627–1717), born in colonial Virginia, the entire American Quesenberry/Quisenberry/Christenberry line descends — every Quesenberry in Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, North Carolina, Texas, and points west. The 1840 federal census found 71% of all Quesenberrys in America still living in Virginia. They all came from this one boy on a Kentish ship in 1624.

There is a poetic detail. Thomas Questenbury's American daughter-in-law, Anne Pope of Westmoreland County (whom his son John married after 1658), was a cousin of another Anne Pope — the Anne Pope who married Colonel John Washington, the great-grandfather of George Washington. The Quesenberrys and the Washingtons share a kinship through the Popes of Westmoreland County.
