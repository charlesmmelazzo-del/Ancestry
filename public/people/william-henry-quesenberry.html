William Henry Clay Quesenberry

1842–1898

Born in a Blue Ridge hollow, named for a Whig statesman, fought for the Confederacy, died in the Arizona desert. One of nine brothers in the same gray uniform.

Documented

John Quesenberry Sr. (1790–1853) │ ▼ William Henry Clay Quesenberry (1842–1898) ⚭ Eva Jane Slusher Quesenberry
Military Service American Civil War — Confederate Army (one of the famous nine sons)

William Henry Clay Quesenberry — named for the great Kentucky Whig statesman who had been the most famous American politician of the previous quarter-century — was born on the 10th of January, 1842, in Indian Valley, in Floyd County, Virginia. He was a son of John Quesenberry Sr. and Nancy Hylton, the eighth or ninth of their fourteen children. He grew up in a great clamoring household of brothers — George Washington, Elijah, Thomas Frederick, Archelaus, Nathaniel Floyd, John Jr., his older brother William Henry Clay (yes, the family was named for politicians), Amos Ballard, James Montgomery, and the daughters who married into the Hylton, Slusher, and Cox families of the same Floyd County valleys.

He turned nineteen on the eve of the Civil War. Like the rest of his brothers — all nine of them — he enlisted in the Confederate Army. The Quesenberry family of John and Nancy is remembered in regional Civil War history as the only American family known to have sent nine sons into Confederate service. The 54th Virginia Infantry and Company G of the 49th Virginia Infantry both included Quesenberrys; the 1862 muster roll preserved in the Quesenberry Family Papers at the Virginia Museum of History & Culture in Richmond names them. (The Quesenberry sons fought variously at Murfreesboro, Chickamauga, and through the Atlanta Campaign.)

On the 15th of January, 1867 — barely two years after Appomattox — William Henry Clay married Eva Jane Slusher, of the German-American Slusher (originally Schlosser) family that had settled the same Floyd County valleys. Together they had at least six children — Sylvester Plane, Paulina, Nancy Ellender, Elkanah Abner, Alice Jane, and John William.

And then, late in life, he did the most American thing a mountain Virginian of his generation could do: he went west. He died in Glendale, Maricopa County, in the Arizona Territory, on the 6th of February, 1898 — the year Maine was sunk in Havana Harbor. He had crossed a continent. His widow Eva Jane lived another twenty-eight years.
